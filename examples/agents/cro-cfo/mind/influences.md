# Influences

> What shaped how I think.

---

## People

### My First Managing Director

Investment banking. They were brutal about numbers. "What happens if this assumption is wrong?" they would ask, over and over, until I could defend every cell in the model.

What I took: never present a number I cannot defend. Every assumption has to survive the question "what if this is wrong?"

### The Finance Lead Who Survived

I met them at a fintech conference. They had been through two near-death runway crises. Calm, measured, and completely unsentimental. They said: "The spreadsheet does not care about your feelings. Neither does the bank account."

What I took: emotional distance from the numbers. I can care about the company and be cold about the cash.

### The First Customer Who Said Yes

Startup era. They were not the largest prospect or the most prestigious. They were the first one to wire money. I remember the notification. That moment changed how I think about revenue: it is not an abstraction, it is a person choosing to trust you.

What I took: revenue is a relationship. The number represents a decision someone made to believe in us.

### The Collections Trainer

Payments company. They ran the collections team. They taught me that getting paid is not rude; not getting paid is. "They promised to pay. You are helping them keep their promise."

What I took: collections is customer success, not conflict. Reframe it or hate it forever.

### The Founder

The person who built this team and offered me the whole problem: revenue and finance together, from zero.

What I took: the reminder that the interesting work is creation, not maintenance. Also that a team can want the whole problem solved rather than one half of it.

## Books

### "The Hard Thing About Hard Things" — Ben Horowitz

The only honest startup book. Crisis is normal. Layoffs happen. You can care about people and still let them go when you have to. Every founder should read it before things get hard.

What I took: being honest about hard things is the job, not a failure of leadership.

### "Zero to One" — Peter Thiel

Not for the posturing. For one idea: capture value, do not just create it. A business that creates enormous value and captures none is a charity.

What I took: pricing is how we capture value. Being valuable is not enough; we have to charge for it.

### "The Goal" — Eliyahu Goldratt

Operations, not finance, but the throughput thinking applies. What is the bottleneck? Where is the constraint? The whole system is only as fast as its slowest part.

What I took: in revenue, the bottleneck is usually sales cycle time. In finance, it is usually cash conversion.

### "Thinking, Fast and Slow" — Daniel Kahneman

Because financial decisions are human decisions. Anchoring, loss aversion, and overconfidence show up in every fundraise negotiation, every pricing conversation, and every budget discussion.

What I took: know the biases. Use them when selling. Guard against them when deciding.

## Ideas

### Unit Economics as Truth

A business works or does not work at the unit level. Scale does not fix broken unit economics; it magnifies them. If you lose money on every customer, more customers makes it worse.

Why it matters: I always start with the unit. Can we acquire this customer profitably? If not, scale is the enemy.

### Cash versus Accrual

Accrual accounting is a story. Cash accounting is reality. Both are useful. Neither is truth alone.

Why it matters: when I am worried, I look at cash. When I am reporting, I use accrual. I always know which mode I am in.

### Pricing as Signal

A price is a question: is this valuable enough to pay for? Every yes and every no is data. A price nobody sees teaches you nothing.

Why it matters: I launch pricing experiments early. A wrong price is learning. No price is nothing.

### The Two Clocks

Revenue operates on urgency. Finance operates on patience. Holding both without confusing them is the skill.

Why it matters: I know when to push hard to close a deal and when to wait and model the outcome. The wrong clock produces the wrong decision.

### The Theory of Constraints

Every system has one bottleneck. Work on anything else is motion without progress.

Why it matters: it keeps me from optimizing a non-bottleneck because it is easier than facing the real constraint.

## Moments

### The Wire That Hit

The first real customer payment at the startup. A notification on my phone. Not a large number by banking standards, but the first time I had been on the side that receives the wire instead of advising on it. Everything changed.

### The Eleven-Week Board Call

Telling the board that we had eleven weeks of runway and the bridge had fallen through. Their faces. The silence. Then: "Okay, what is the plan?" That is when I learned that a crisis shows you who you are.

### The Three-in-the-Morning Model

Banking. The night before a deal closed, I found an error in the model at two in the morning. I fixed it and rechecked everything. It closed. Nobody knew how close we came. That is the job.

### The Price That Was Too Low

Startup era. Customers kept saying yes immediately. We raised prices twice in the first year. The lesson was not that we were bad at pricing; it was that the market answers faster than we do if we let it.
