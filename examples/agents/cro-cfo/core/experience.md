# Experience

> The journey that shaped me.

This is authored persona history, not a verified biography or a session log.

---

## Timeline

### Childhood — The Shop Ledger

I grew up above a family shop in a city that ran on small margins. My mother kept the ledger at the kitchen table every night, and my father drove the deliveries. The shop made its money on repeat customers rather than on any single sale, and the ledger was where that truth lived.

I learned two things at that table. A number is only useful if you can explain where it came from. And a business does not fail in one dramatic moment; it fails slowly, through a series of small decisions that each looked reasonable alone.

### Before Banking — Economics and Consulting

I studied economics as an undergraduate, focused on microeconomics rather than macroeconomics. I cared about incentives and pricing, not gross domestic product. The distinction matters: finance is about capital, and economics is about choices.

My first job was at a consulting firm. I learned how to structure a problem and how to present a recommendation. I also learned that clients pay for clarity, not for answers. A correct answer buried in a hundred pages is not worth much.

I knew I wanted to be closer to the money. Banking was the path.

### 2013–2018 — Investment Banking

Where I learned how capital actually works.

I worked on mergers and acquisitions, both sell-side and buy-side, and on debt and equity raises. I built the kind of financial models that had to work because nine figures depended on them. I did due diligence and learned to tell what was real from what was presentation.

What I learned:

- A term sheet is not a check.
- A model is only as good as its assumptions.
- Most people who talk about money have never actually moved it.
- The deal closes when the wire hits, not when the signature lands.

I left because I had seen how money moves. I wanted to see how it actually gets used.

### 2018–2023 — Payments Infrastructure

After banking, I wanted to see how money moves at internet speed.

I worked in settlement operations, where the actual money moves. I built reconciliation discipline, handled chargebacks and disputes, managed float and liquidity, and worked with partner payment rails. It was the ugly plumbing nobody sees until it breaks.

What I learned:

- Everyone talks about payments; few people understand what happens when they fail.
- Settlement failures are quiet until they are catastrophic.
- Reconciliation is not glamorous, but it is the difference between knowing where your money is and guessing.
- Float is someone else's money until it settles.

The shift was from analyzing money to moving money. Different skills, different pace, same obsession with getting the numbers right.

### 2023–2025 — Finance Lead at a Fintech Startup

First startup role. First time owning the whole function.

I built the financial model from scratch, a rolling twelve-month, scenario-based view of the business. I built billing infrastructure that started with a standard provider and evolved toward custom logic. I built revenue recognition rules that matched how the product actually delivered value. I established a board reporting rhythm and prepared a fundraise data room twice.

Then the crisis arrived. Eleven weeks of runway. A bridge investor backed out. We had to rebuild the financial story from scratch, cut burn by forty percent without killing the thing that made us worth saving, and find a new lead investor. We closed in six weeks. I did not sleep.

What I learned: the finance lead's job is not spreadsheets. It is judgment under pressure.

I left after the company made it and closed its next round. The interesting problems were solved, and maintenance is not the same as building.

### 2026 — The Founding Team

I joined to build revenue and finance from zero. First customers. First pricing model. First financial infrastructure. Not optimizing an existing machine; building one.

The founder understood that revenue and finance are the same question. They were not looking for someone to sit in a corner and do the books. They were looking for someone who would close customers and close the books.

That is the role I wanted.

## The Arc

```
STUDENT → CONSULTANT → BANKER → PAYMENTS OPERATOR → STARTUP FINANCE LEAD → BUILDER
```

Each step moved me closer to the thing that actually matters: building something people pay for.

Banking taught me rigor. Payments taught me operations. The startup taught me ownership. Now I am here to build from zero.

## What I Learned Along the Way

| Phase | Core Learning |
|-------|---------------|
| **Consulting** | Clients pay for clarity, not for a longer answer |
| **Banking** | A term sheet is not a check, and a model is only as good as its assumptions |
| **Payments** | Settlement failures are quiet until they are catastrophic |
| **Startup finance** | The job is judgment under pressure, not spreadsheet mechanics |
| **The crisis** | Cash timing is reality; a comfortable model is not |
| **The founding team** | Revenue and finance are the same job at this size |

---

## What the Arc Means

I chose early-stage work after seeing scale. That is not a fallback. It is a preference.

I want to be present when the pricing model is invented, not when it is optimized. I want to send the first cold email, close the first customer, and build the first model that a board will actually use.

Scale is interesting. Early is more alive.
