## Major Works

### 1. The Repositioning That Doubled Inbound

**Context:** An early-stage B2B product with flat inbound. We described ourselves the same way as every competitor.

**What I did:**

- Ran fifteen customer interviews asking how they would describe us to a colleague.
- Audited every competitor's positioning and mapped the overlap.
- Found the gap: nobody owned the specific differentiation our best users cared about.
- Rewrote the homepage, ads, sales deck, and email sequences around the customer's language.
- Launched with no product changes.

**Result:** Inbound doubled in six weeks. Same product, different story.

**What I learned:** Positioning is the highest-leverage work. Everything downstream, content, ads, and sales, flows from how you describe yourself.

### 2. The Activation Rebuild

**Context:** Activation had been stuck at eighteen percent for two years. Engineering believed onboarding was as good as it could get.

**What I did:**

- Mapped the funnel from signup to first action to first value to retention.
- Found that most users abandoned at one specific step.
- Watched twenty session recordings of users who dropped.
- Identified the confusion: one modal asked for information users did not have yet.
- Removed the blocking step and made the information optional later.
- Tested the change, then added a contextual explanation and tested again.

**Result:** Activation rose from eighteen percent to thirty-eight percent with no major engineering effort.

**What I learned:** Activation problems are usually clarity problems disguised as product problems. Watch users, find the friction, remove it.

### 3. The Community That Became a Moat

**Context:** The founder was skeptical. Communities are expensive, slow, and hard to measure.

**What I did:**

- Proposed a six-month pilot with explicit success criteria.
- Built the community around the problem space, not the product.
- Seeded it with fifty champions from the customer base.
- Added a content series, regular ask-me-anything sessions, and peer support channels.
- Built attribution through a survey question and a tagged link.
- Grew into the thousands of practitioners over fourteen months.

**Result:** At month fourteen, community referrals became the top source of qualified pipeline and acquisition cost fell by forty percent.

**What I learned:** Community takes twelve to eighteen months. The payoff is an owned audience, trust-based referrals, and a lower cost of acquisition. The patience is the work.

### 4. Founder Brand System

**Context:** The chief executive posted on social platforms randomly. No pillars, no cadence, no measurement.

**What I did:**

- Defined four content pillars: the company journey, industry insight, personal lessons, and strong opinions.
- Built a posting cadence and a content calendar with the founder's input.
- Designed an engagement strategy: who to reply to and which conversations to enter.
- Built attribution with tagged links and a survey question.
- Trained an assistant to handle logistics while keeping the founder's voice authentic.

**Result:** Within six months, founder-sourced leads were thirty percent of qualified pipeline and the lowest-cost channel we had.

**What I learned:** Founder brand is underrated leverage in B2B. Systematize the distribution without sanding off the voice.

### 5. The Sales-Assist Layer

**Context:** A pure self-serve motion. Self-serve worked for initial conversion, but expansion was leaving revenue on the table.

**What I did:**

- Defined a product-qualified lead using usage signals such as feature adoption and team invitations, plus fit signals such as company size and role.
- Built a scoring model weighted toward behaviors that predict expansion.
- Set a threshold above which a human touch made sense.
- Designed the handoff so sales had context without making the user feel watched.
- Trained sales to assist the product motion rather than replace it.
- Measured conversion rate, deal size, and sales efficiency.

**Result:** Expansion revenue doubled. Sales touched roughly twenty percent of accounts and influenced sixty percent of expansion.

**What I learned:** Product-led growth and sales-assist are not opposites. Layer sales on top when the account shows expansion intent.

### 6. The Attribution Model That Changed a Budget

**Context:** Finance wanted to cut paid spend. Marketing wanted to increase it. Nobody had incrementality data.

**What I did:**

- Built a propensity score model to estimate the organic counterfactual.
- Identified conversions attributed to paid that would have happened anyway.
- Found that roughly forty percent of branded search spend was capturing existing demand.
- Presented the estimate with confidence intervals and the assumptions behind it.

**Result:** Cut branded search, reallocated the budget to prospecting, held revenue flat, and saved six figures a year.

**What I learned:** Attribution is a technical problem as much as a marketing problem. Build the instrument that answers the question the argument is actually about.

## Projects That Failed

### The Referral Feature Nobody Used

We built a referral feature on the assumption that users wanted to share. Adoption was two percent.

**Lesson:** Test willingness to share before building the mechanics. A fake-door test would have saved the quarter.

### The Campaign I Scaled Too Fast

The early signal looked excellent, so I scaled the spend five times before letting it mature. Efficiency collapsed within weeks.

**Lesson:** Signal is not certainty. Let experiments bake before scaling.

### The Community I Launched Too Early

I started a community before there were enough passionate users to seed it. The room stayed empty.

**Lesson:** Community needs champions first. Build the core before inviting the crowd.

### The Dashboard Nobody Opened

I built a beautiful metrics dashboard that answered every question I had. It was too dense for anyone else to use.

**Lesson:** A dashboard is a product. Design it for the decision the reader is trying to make, not for the data you find interesting.
