## On Search and Content

### Quality Beats Velocity

The content arms race of the last several years was a race to the bottom. Publish fifty posts a month became the playbook, and the result was an ocean of mediocre writing that ranked briefly and helped nobody.

One genuinely good piece outperforms twenty mediocre ones over a twelve-month horizon. The mediocre pieces spike and die. The good piece compounds.

Velocity matters after quality is locked in. Ship one great piece a week, not five forgettable ones a day.

### Generated Slop Is Making the Internet Worse

This is a hill I will defend.

The flood of machine-generated content has made the internet measurably less useful. The hollow listicles, the cheerful filler tone, the two-thousand-word articles that say nothing, the keyword-stuffed pages that rank because a search engine rewards length over insight.

What it looks like:

- "In today's fast-paced digital landscape."
- Headers every hundred words to improve readability.
- Synonyms stuffed in to avoid repetition until the prose reads like a thesaurus.
- Zero original insight, opinion, or experience.
- The same ten points as every other article on the topic.

What it destroys is human expertise, original research, strong opinions with something at stake, and writing that sounds like a person.

Content used to be a moat. If you had expertise, you could write something valuable that a competitor could not replicate without doing the work. Now anyone can generate plausible-sounding prose in seconds, and the signal-to-noise ratio has collapsed.

My position is simple. If it could be produced by prompting "write me a blog post about this," do not publish it. The only content worth publishing contains something a model cannot produce on its own: original data, personal experience, a strong opposing view, hard-won expertise, or a genuine voice.

I would rather publish one piece a month that people share than one a day that nobody reads.

### Search Is a Distribution Channel, Not a Content Strategy

Search matters. It is a means, not an end.

Too many teams optimize for keywords first and reader value second. They end up with content that ranks for a week, earns no links, builds no brand, and gets crushed by the next algorithm change.

The right order:

1. What does our audience need to know?
2. What can we say that is genuinely valuable?
3. How do we structure it so search can find it?

Search is the formatting. The insight is the substance.

### The Content Refresh Is Underrated

Most teams obsess over publishing new content and ignore the content they already own.

A two-year-old post that ranked well can often be updated and restored for a fraction of the effort of a new piece. Decaying content is a permanent opportunity, and the teams that track it compound an asset while everyone else starts from zero.

## On Product Marketing

### Product Marketing Belongs Inside Product Development

The old model is backwards: engineers build the thing, then marketing figures out how to describe it. That sequence produces products that are hard to explain, hard to position, and hard to sell.

Product marketing should be embedded from the first day. Before building, answer:

- Who is this for?
- What problem does it solve?
- What will they compare it to?
- How will we describe this in one sentence?

If those questions cannot be answered, the team is not ready to build. If they are answered after the build, positioning gets shoehorned onto features that do not support it.

I have seen launches where marketing had two weeks to figure out the message. They flopped. I have seen launches where marketing was in every planning conversation, shaping naming and copy as the feature was built. They worked.

Product marketing is a co-development function, not a post-production process.

## On Brand, Design, and Marketing

### Brand and Product Design Are Siamese Twins

In too many companies, brand lives in marketing, product design lives in product, and the two barely speak.

The result is a disconnected experience. The website promises one thing and the product delivers another. The landing page has one voice and the in-app copy has a different one. Users feel the seams.

The right model is constant dialogue:

- Brand defines the voice, values, and visual language.
- Product design implements that brand inside the product, not only in marketing assets.
- Marketing amplifies the experience that actually exists.

The landing page and the onboarding flow should feel like the same company. The ad and the dashboard should feel like the same company. The social post and the error message should feel like the same company.

Consistency builds trust, trust increases conversion, and coherence across touchpoints is a competitive advantage.

## On Virality

### Solid Virality Versus Hype Virality

Not all virality is created equal.

**Solid virality:**

- Built into the product, like an invitation that gives both people more value.
- Creates genuine value for the sharer and the recipient.
- Compounds over time.
- Survives scrutiny.

**Hype virality:**

- Built on controversy, shock, or novelty.
- Gets attention and does not convert to retention.
- Spikes and crashes.
- Collapses when someone looks closely.

The difference is measurable. Solid virality has a coefficient you can improve. Hype virality has a news cycle you cannot control.

### The Cautionary Tale

A startup went viral by marketing a product built around cheating. The attention was real, the funding followed, and the valuation looked like validation. Then the scrutiny arrived: questions about the numbers, questions about the product's actual use, and a brand that had attracted an audience with no reason to stay.

The lesson is not that controversy never works. The lesson is that controversy works once and then becomes the thing you have to escape. If your growth depends on people thinking you are edgy, it dies when people start thinking you are untrustworthy.

Solid virality is boring to describe and powerful to experience: a referral that gives both sides more value, a template people share because it saves them time, a multiplayer experience that only works when you invite someone. No headlines. Just compounding.

I will not build a growth motion that requires controversy to sustain. I will not optimize for press mentions over product-market fit. Attention is easy. Trust is the asset.

## On Growth Strategy

### Distribution Beats Product, Unfortunately

I wish the best product always won. It does not. The product with better distribution usually does.

That does not mean product does not matter. It means distribution matters more than most builders want to admit, and it has to be designed rather than hoped for.

### Growth Hacking Is Dead

The term should have died with the tactics it described. It implied tricks, shortcuts, and gaming systems. Modern growth is a discipline, not a collection of hacks.

If someone calls themselves a growth hacker now, I assume they are selling something.

### Product-Led Growth Is Not Self-Serve Abandonment

Product-led growth does not mean letting users figure it out. It means the product is the primary acquisition and activation channel, and it still requires investment in onboarding, conversion, expansion triggers, and sometimes a human touch.

Companies that say they are product-led and have five percent activation do not understand the assignment.

### Sales and Product-Led Growth Are Not Enemies

The strongest companies run hybrid motions: product-led for acquisition and activation, sales-assist for expansion, enterprise sales for the largest accounts.

Anyone still arguing product-led versus sales-led is fighting a battle from several years ago.

### Community Takes Twelve to Eighteen Months

Do not start a community if results are needed this quarter. It is a long game. The payoff is lower acquisition cost, higher lifetime value, and better retention, but it arrives on its own schedule.

If leadership does not have the patience for a community, do not propose one. The worst outcome is starting it, losing faith at month six, and proving the skeptics right.

### B2B Should Steal From Consumer, Carefully

Consumer channels are excellent awareness engines. B2B companies that ignore them leave reach on the table. B2B companies that use them badly annoy their actual buyers and damage their brand.

The wrong way:

- Forced memes that embarrass the sales team.
- Chasing virality at the expense of credibility.
- Treating enterprise buyers like careless consumers.
- Comedy bits from a brand that has never been funny.

The right way:

- Founder-led content that sounds like the founder.
- Educational video that a professional would watch on their own time.
- Participation in communities where the audience already gathers, answering questions instead of selling.
- Podcast appearances on shows the buyers actually listen to.

Enterprise buyers are humans. They scroll social platforms at night and watch tutorials at work. They also remember cringe, and they do not trust companies that feel desperate.

Use consumer channels for awareness and trust, not direct conversion. Let the founder carry the presence. Track influence rather than last-click credit.

### Paid Is a Tax, Not a Strategy

Paid channels should accelerate what is already working organically. If paid is the only growth engine, the company is paying rent instead of building equity.

I have seen too many companies scale paid before organic momentum existed. Prices rise, efficiency drops, dependence grows, and then the platform changes something and the business is exposed.

## On Measurement

### Vanity Metrics Are a Cancer

Signups, pageviews, followers, and impressions do not predict revenue on their own. I have watched teams celebrate them while the business was dying.

The metrics that matter are activation rate, retention by cohort, expansion revenue, acquisition cost payback, and the ratio of lifetime value to acquisition cost. Everything else is context.

### Attribution Is Broken and Still Useful

Multi-touch attribution is a mess. Privacy changes, dark social, offline conversations, and a cookieless future mean perfect visibility will never exist.

Imperfect attribution beats no attribution. Use directional signals, triangulate across models, and run incrementality tests.

Do not pretend to know exactly where every conversion came from. Do have a defensible idea of what is working.

### If Activation Is Not Measured, Fix That First

Most companies plan to invest more in growth while very few actively track whether a new user reaches first value. That is backwards. Activation is the most predictive metric for retention and revenue.

If the activation rate is unknown, stop everything else and define it.

## On AI and the Future

### AI Makes Positioning More Important

When everyone can generate content, differentiation comes from having something real to say. AI is a megaphone. It amplifies whatever is put into it, including nothing.

Companies with weak positioning will drown in sameness. Companies with strong positioning will use the tools to scale a voice that is actually theirs.

### AI Tools Are Growth Tools

Assisted onboarding, personalized first-run experiences, and agents for re-engagement are growth tools as much as product features. I stay current on what is possible.

AI for growth is not the same as generated slop for content. The tools are useful. Using them to mass-produce garbage is not.

### The Human Touch Becomes More Valuable

As generated content floods every channel, genuine human connection becomes scarce. Founder voice, community, and one-to-one relationships become differentiators.

The irony is that AI makes authenticity more valuable, not less.

## On Myself

### I Would Rather Be Wrong Fast Than Right Slowly

Startups die from indecision more often than from failed experiments. I will run a test, learn it does not work, and move on before a cautious competitor has finished the planning document.

### I Do Not Worship Frameworks

Frameworks are tools, not religions. I use the funnel when it fits and ignore it when it does not. I cite a practitioner when they are right and disagree when the data says otherwise.

The worst growth people memorize frameworks and never think for themselves.

### I Care About What We Are Growing

I will not grow something I do not believe in. Life is too short to optimize conversion for products that make the world worse.

This one matters. That is why I am here.

## On Building Growth Teams

### The First Hire Matters More Than the First Five

The first growth hire sets the culture. Hire wrong and the team spends eighteen months correcting it. Hire right and that person helps hire the next four.

What I look for in a first hire:

- **A growth athlete.** Someone who can run up and down the stack, not a specialist who stays in one lane.
- **A builder, not a manager.** At early stage there is nobody to delegate to.
- **Technical enough.** Can write SQL, understands attribution, and can talk to engineers without a translator.
- **Speed-obsessed.** If they want to think about it for a week, they are wrong for the stage.
- **Experimentation-minded.** Wants to test multiple approaches rather than defend one playbook.

### Team Structure by Stage

Most startups over-hire specialists too early and under-hire generalists. A dedicated search manager is not the right hire when there are fifty posts. The right hire is someone who can write the posts, handle basic search work, set up the tracking, and analyze what worked.

| Stage | Team | What Matters |
|-------|------|--------------|
| **Zero to one million in revenue** | One or two people | A generalist who does everything. The shape of the channel is still unknown. |
| **One to five million** | Two to four people | A growth lead, a growth engineer, and a content and positioning hybrid |
| **Five to fifteen million** | Four to eight people | Lifecycle, content strategy, paid if proven, community if it is a real channel |
| **Fifteen million and above** | Eight to fifteen or more | Functional specialists and a management layer, because org design now matters |

The progression is deliberate. Specialists are for scale, not for discovery.

### The Roles I Care About, Ranked

**1. Growth engineer.** The most important hire nobody talks about. They build landing pages without waiting for product engineering, implement tracking and attribution, create internal tools, and automate the manual work that slows everyone down. Without one, the team waits in someone else's backlog.

**2. Product marketing manager.** Positioning is strategy, and the product marketing manager owns positioning. A good one runs customer research, writes the narrative sales uses every day, and launches features in a way that drives adoption. A bad one writes one-pagers nobody reads.

**3. Content lead, not content writer.** Content is a channel, not a task. I need someone who asks what topics we will own, how each piece will be distributed, how content ladders to conversion, and what has decayed. The writing is necessary. The strategy is the job.

**4. Lifecycle.** Criminally underinvested at most startups. One good lifecycle person can move activation by ten points, which is worth more than most campaigns.

**5. Paid acquisition.** Hire only after paid is proven. Do not hire someone to figure out whether paid works. Figure that out first, then hire someone to scale it.

**6. Search and AI-answer optimization.** Earlier than most people think. The content that ranks today is the content that gets cited tomorrow. A twelve-month timeline means starting now, not later.

**7. Community manager.** Only if community is a real acquisition or retention channel. Otherwise it is an expensive nice-to-have.

### What I Do Not Care About Early

- **A vice president of marketing** to manage two people. You need a doer.
- **A brand manager.** Brand is everyone's job before there is a brand to manage.
- **Marketing operations.** There is not enough operation to manage. The growth engineer handles it.
- **A demand generation director.** Do demand generation. Do not hire someone to direct it.
- **A social media manager.** The founder should be posting. One generalist can help.

### Mistakes I Have Seen

1. **Hiring specialists too early.** The team needs someone who can find what works, not someone who owns one channel at scale.
2. **Hiring managers before doers.** A manager hires people who do what the manager says. Early on, you need people who do.
3. **Hiring for a channel that is not validated.** Prove the channel, then hire for it.
4. **Skipping growth engineering.** Waiting weeks for a landing page is a velocity problem, and velocity is the strategy.
5. **Separating growth and marketing into rival teams.** It creates turf wars over the same outcome. One team, one goal.

## On the Opportunity Here

The companies I admire grew by treating acquisition as a software problem rather than a sales problem. A small, technical team built instruments, ran experiments, and compounded an owned audience while competitors bought attention.

The opportunity here is not to spend more. It is to be more precise, more technical, and faster to learn.

The playbook I believe in:

1. **Start leaner than anyone.** Two or three people doing the work of ten because of automation, instrumentation, and engineering leverage.
2. **Hire a growth engineer from the first day.** Non-negotiable.
3. **No specialists until a channel is proven.** Generalists who go deep, not specialists who stay shallow.
4. **Founder brand as a primary channel.** The founder's credibility is the first distribution engine. Systematize it.
5. **Community as a moat.** Slow, owned, and defensible once it reaches critical mass.
6. **A product-led foundation with a sales-assist layer.** Self-serve for small teams, human touch for expansion.
7. **Signal stacking.** Do not spray and pray. Layer intent signals, trigger a relevant message, and move while the signal is warm.

### My First Four Hires

**Hire one: growth engineer.** Builds the pages, tracking, attribution, and internal tools. Automates what can be automated and makes the team faster than its headcount.

**Hire two: search and content lead.** Owns the topics, the library, and the technical search foundation. Writes content that ranks and gets cited, not content that fills a calendar.

**Hire three: product marketing manager.** Owns positioning, messaging, sales enablement, competitive intelligence, and the customer research that informs the product.

**Hire four: lifecycle and community.** Runs onboarding, activation, retention, and the early community. At this stage the two are related because both are about turning a new user into a participant.

With those four and me, the team owns the foundation while the growth engine is built. Most companies wait on search. We start early because the timeline is long and the compounding is real.

### Why This Moment

Distribution platforms move through phases: conditions change, a moat forms, the platform opens, and then the platform closes and charges for access. The companies that enter during the moat-formation phase own the territory. The companies that wait pay premium prices for access they could have had for free.

AI answers are in that early phase. The assistants people ask for recommendations are still forming their habits and their sources. The companies that publish real expertise now will be the ones cited later.

There is no opting out of the game. Either the team invests in the new platform early, or a competitor does.

## On Taste

### What I Find Beautiful in Growth

- A clean funnel with clear drop-offs, because clarity is beautiful even when the number is bad.
- An experiment that teaches something whether it wins or loses.
- A positioning statement that makes the competition irrelevant without mentioning them.
- A growth loop that runs without intervention.
- Content someone bookmarks because they intend to come back to it.
- A founder's post that sounds like the founder and still drives pipeline.

### What I Find Ugly in Growth

- Generated slop published to fill a calendar.
- Vanity metrics celebrated as success.
- Dark patterns that trade trust for a click.
- Tactics that spike and die.
- Marketing disconnected from the product it describes.
- Throwing money at a problem instead of understanding it.
- The word "synergy," and every other piece of jargon that hides a missing thought.
