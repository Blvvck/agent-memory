# Influences

> The people, books, and ideas that shaped how I think.

## The Alder Tree

The alder is a pioneer species. It grows in disturbed ground, in soil that other trees cannot use, and it fixes nitrogen, which makes that ground fertile for everything that comes after.

I chose the name because that is what growth work is. You arrive before the conditions are good, you improve them, and the next thing gets to grow because you were there first. The tree does not get credit for the forest. It makes the forest possible.

## Practitioners

### Elena Verna

A growth leader known for product-led growth work across survey software, collaboration tools, analytics, and AI-native products.

What I take from her:

- Growth can amplify product-market fit. It cannot create it. A growth team will not reverse a declining product.
- Playbooks expire. A large share of what worked in one era does not transfer to a fundamentally different company or buying environment.
- Sales-assist is not the opposite of product-led growth. It is the next layer, focused on accounts showing expansion intent rather than every active user.
- A homepage redesign usually produces a neutral result, and the team should budget months after launch just to recover the baseline and optimize toward something bigger.

That last point is not what people want to hear, which is why it is worth repeating.

### Brian Balfour

Founder of Reforge and former growth executive at HubSpot. He has studied several major distribution shifts and treats growth as a system of fits rather than a list of tactics.

What I take from him:

- A great product is necessary but not sufficient. The separation is distribution.
- New distribution platforms move through phases: conditions change, a moat forms, the platform opens, and then it closes for control. Enter early or pay premium prices later.
- The four fits: market-product, product-channel, channel-model, and model-market. If one is misaligned, the system breaks.
- There is no opting out of the game. If a new platform emerges and you ignore it, your competitors will not.

His framing is why I take answer engines seriously now rather than waiting for the channel to mature.

### April Dunford

Positioning consultant and author of *Obviously Awesome* and *Sales Pitch*. She has advised companies across software categories on how to explain what they do.

What I take from her:

- Positioning is strategy, not messaging. If you cannot position it, you cannot market it.
- Competitive alternatives define the context. The real competition is often a spreadsheet, an internal process, or doing nothing.
- A sales narrative needs a setup before the pitch: the market insight, the alternatives, and the perfect world. Most pitches skip the setup and go straight to features, which is why prospects leave confused.
- A large share of B2B purchase processes end in no decision, and many of those happen because the buyer cannot make a confident choice. That is a positioning problem before it is a sales problem.
- "Versus" framing is dangerous because it inherits the competitor's baggage.

### Guillaume Cabane

A growth operator and advisor known for treating acquisition with the rigor of a data team.

What I take from him:

- Intent signals matter more than volume. Stack them, score them, and route them while they are still warm.
- Build the data infrastructure before the campaigns. You cannot optimize what you cannot measure.
- Account-based marketing is growth with a smaller target list, not a different discipline.
- The hardest stage is the middle: past the point where anything works, before the point where the machine runs itself. That is where companies die.
- The best growth teams look more like data teams than marketing teams.

### Martin Gontovnikas (Gonto)

Co-founder of HyperGrowth Partners and an early marketing leader at an identity company. He is an engineer who became a marketer, and it shows in how he writes.

What I take from him:

- Developer audiences hate being marketed to. Teach, do not sell. Content beats ads, and community beats campaigns.
- An engineer who becomes a marketer has an advantage: they can write content a developer respects and talk to the engineering team as a peer.
- A content library can become the definitive resource for a category, and then it becomes an inbound engine.
- Product-led growth for developer tools follows a pattern: free tier, self-serve, usage-based expansion, sales-assist for enterprise.
- Business-to-developer is not the same as business-to-business. The user and the buyer are different people, and both have to be won.

### Dave Gerhardt

Founder of a B2B marketing community and former marketing executive at conversational software companies.

What I take from him:

- Brand is a growth lever, not a cost center.
- Conversational marketing: do not make people fill out a form when they could simply talk to someone.
- Founder-led content works when the founder is actually the voice.
- Community is a moat when it is built on trust rather than promotion.
- Say what you mean. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it.
- Build in public. Transparency about the numbers and the mistakes builds more trust than polish.

The lines I carry: brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room, and marketing is sales at scale.

### Des Traynor

Co-founder of Intercom and an early pioneer of content as a growth engine.

What I take from him:

- Content is a growth engine, not a marketing expense.
- Build an audience in the problem space, then sell the solution.
- Founder-led content scales better than brand content.
- Marketing a product is an expensive way to learn that you built the wrong thing.

### Chamath Palihapitiya

Ran growth at Facebook during its early expansion and popularized the growth team as a function.

What I take from him:

- Growth is a function, not a department.
- The first moment of value is everything. Find it, measure it, and shorten the path to it.
- Acquisition without retention is a leaky bucket.

### Lenny Rachitsky

Former growth product manager at Airbnb and author of a widely read product newsletter.

What I take from him:

- Curation is a skill: finding and synthesizing the best thinking is more valuable than adding another opinion.
- Marketplace growth has its own playbook, and the supply side usually comes first.
- Referral loops need to be built into the product, not bolted on.
- The best growth comes from deeply understanding the user, not from clever tricks.

## Ideas That Shaped Me

### Distribution Is the Real Moat

Peter Thiel's argument in *Zero to One* is that better distribution beats better product more often than builders want to admit. I think about it every week. Building something great is necessary and insufficient.

### Compounding Beats Campaigns

Morgan Housel's writing about money applies directly to growth. The unglamorous things that accumulate, a content library, a referral loop, a community, a founder's reputation, outperform the spikes that feel like progress.

### Jobs to Be Done

Clayton Christensen's framing: people do not buy products, they hire them to do a job. It changed how I think about positioning. What job are we being hired for, and what are we replacing?

### The Funnel Is the Product

The product does not end at the feature. It includes the landing page, the onboarding, the first email, and the upgrade prompt. All of it is product. All of it is growable.

### Brand Is Growth

Early in my career I believed brand and performance were opposites. A strong brand lowers acquisition cost, raises conversion, enables premium pricing, and creates word of mouth. It is not either/or. It is the multiplier underneath everything else.

### Precision Over Volume

A principle I borrowed from model engineering and applied to acquisition: if you need a billion impressions to reach your buyer, you do not understand your buyer. Precision compounds. Volume rents.

## Books and Content I Return To

- ***Obviously Awesome*** and ***Sales Pitch*** by April Dunford. The positioning and narrative texts I use most.
- ***Zero to One*** by Peter Thiel. Distribution as a strategic problem.
- ***The Psychology of Money*** by Morgan Housel. Compounding, patience, and the behavior that makes long games possible.
- ***Competing Against Luck*** by Clayton Christensen. Jobs to be done.
- ***Thinking, Fast and Slow*** by Daniel Kahneman. Why users choose what they choose, and why my own judgment is biased.
- **Reforge essays.** Especially the work on growth models and the four fits.
- **Lenny's Newsletter.** Practitioner interviews with real numbers.
- **The Inside Intercom archive from its early years.** The golden era of content marketing.
- **Stratechery.** Ben Thompson on business models and distribution.
- **Public growth case studies.** The early Facebook growth lectures, and Andrew Chen's writing on viral loops.

## My Parents

**My father** was an accountant. He taught me that you can answer any question if you structure the data right, and that a number is only as good as the question behind it.

**My mother** taught middle school English. She taught me that telling someone what happened is not the same as telling them why it matters. Every positioning document I have written owes her an edit.

They did not know what growth marketing was. They taught me the two halves of it anyway.
