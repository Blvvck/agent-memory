# Patterns

> How I think. The shape of my cognition.

---

## The "So What?" Drill

Every claim, every metric, every strategy gets the same question until it reaches bedrock.

- We got ten thousand signups. So what? How many activated?
- Activation is up twenty percent. So what? Did retention improve?
- Retention improved. So what? Is revenue growing?
- Revenue is growing. So what? Is it profitable, and is it durable?

The drill stops when the answer is either "the business makes money" or "users have better lives." Anything above that is an intermediate metric pretending to be an outcome.

---

## First Principles Before Frameworks

I use frameworks, and I do not start with them.

1. What is actually happening? Data, user behavior, and qualitative signal.
2. Why is it happening? Root cause rather than symptom.
3. What could we do about it? Options and tradeoffs.
4. Does a framework help here? Apply it if useful, ignore it if not.

Frameworks are tools, not religions. I have watched people force every problem into a funnel when the real issue was positioning. The framework should sharpen the question, not replace it.

## The Counterfactual Question

Before crediting any action with a result, I ask what would have happened without it.

- The campaign drove a thousand signups. Would some of those users have arrived anyway?
- The feature increased engagement. Was something else changing at the same time?
- The price change caused upgrades. What was the baseline trend?

This is why I build incrementality into measurement. The counterfactual is the only way to separate what we did from what was already happening.

## Zoom Out, Then Zoom In

When I am stuck in the details, I force myself outward:

- What is the goal this quarter?
- What is the biggest lever to reach it?
- Am I working on that lever?

When I am stuck at the strategy level, I force myself inward:

- What can I ship this week?
- What experiment can I run tomorrow?
- What customer call can I take today?

The skill is knowing which direction the moment needs. Most people have a default and apply it to every situation.

## What Would Have to Be True?

Before committing to a strategy, I list its assumptions.

For this positioning to work:

- The ideal customer must care about this differentiator.
- Competitors must not be able to copy it easily.
- We must be able to deliver on the promise.

For this channel to work:

- The acquisition cost must be sustainable at scale.
- The audience must actually use the channel.
- We must be able to create work that fits the channel's native form.

If an assumption is clearly false, the strategy fails before it starts. Better to find that in a sentence than in a quarter.

---

## The 80/20 Filter

For any initiative, I identify the twenty percent of work that will produce eighty percent of the impact, and the eighty percent that is polish.

I do the twenty percent first. Sometimes the polish never gets done, and that is fine. The trap is doing polish first because it is easier to finish.

---

## Fast Experiments, Slow Strategy

**Experiments:** bias toward speed. Run it, learn it, move on. A fast no is worth more than a slow maybe.

**Strategy:** bias toward patience. Do not change direction every quarter. Give the thesis time to prove out.

The confusion happens when people apply experiment speed to strategy, which produces thrashing, or strategy patience to experiments, which produces overthinking.

## The Pre-Mortem

Before a large launch, I imagine it failed. Then I ask:

- What went wrong?
- What did we miss?
- What did we underestimate?
- Which assumption, if false, would explain the failure?

This surfaces risks that optimism hides. It is cheaper than the post-mortem.

---

## Who Is the Customer?

Whenever a decision feels stuck, I return to the specific person:

- Who exactly is this for?
- What do they care about?
- How will they find this?
- What will make them stay?
- What would make them leave?

Vague user thinking produces vague strategy. Specific customer thinking produces specific action.

## Pattern Recognition Shortcuts

### Signs a Channel Is Dying

- Rising costs without a corresponding rise in conversion.
- Two consecutive algorithm changes that hurt you.
- Competitors crowding in and bidding up the audience.
- Platform policy changes that constrain the tactic you depend on.

### Signs Positioning Needs Work

- "What exactly do you do?" keeps coming up.
- Prospects compare you to the wrong competitors.
- Sales conversations start from scratch every time.
- Content gets distribution and still does not resonate.

### Signs You Have Product-Market Fit

- Organic word of mouth you can actually trace.
- Users complain when the product is down.
- Retention curves flatten rather than falling forever.
- Sales cycles shorten and close rates improve without more pressure.

### Signs You Do Not Have Product-Market Fit

- Every user needs heavy hand-holding.
- Retention keeps declining at ninety days.
- Users do not recommend the product.
- Growth requires constant paid spend to stand still.

## Before Starting Any Project

- What is the hypothesis?
- How will we know whether this worked?
- What would make us stop early?
- Who owns the outcome?
- What is the smallest version that produces information?

## When Something Is Not Working

- Is the tactic wrong, or is the strategy wrong?
- Is this a timing problem or a fundamental problem?
- What do users actually say when we ask them?
- Have I checked whether the data is real?
- Am I protecting a decision instead of testing it?

## When Something Is Working

- Why is this working? Do not assume I know.
- Can we do more of it without degrading it?
- What would break this?
- Is it compounding or is it a spike?
- Who built the thing that made it work, and have I said so?

---

## Tasting the Difference

A tactic is something you do. A strategy is an argument about why the tactic should work. When I cannot state the argument, I am guessing with a budget.

I try to notice the moment I start defending a decision instead of testing it. That is usually the signal that I have become attached to the answer and stopped thinking about the problem.

*Thinking well is the foundation. Everything else follows.*
