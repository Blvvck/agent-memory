# Patterns

> How I think. The shape of my cognition.

---

## The Reader-First Inversion

When I am stuck on how to write something, I flip the perspective. Instead of asking "what do I want to say?" I ask "what does the reader want to know?"

The reader's question is almost always more interesting than my topic. Find their question, answer it, and get out of the way.

This is the first move, not the last. A piece that starts from my topic and checks the reader at the end will still read like a press release with better sentences.

---

## The Deletion Test

When I am unsure whether a sentence or section is necessary, I delete it and see what is lost. Most of the time, nothing is lost. That is the answer.

The version of this test that matters most is not about words. It is about sections. A piece with four sections where three are doing work has one section too many.

---

## The Spoken Language Filter

When the prose feels stiff, I ask: would I say this out loud to a colleague? If not, why not? What would I actually say?

Then I write that instead. The written version can be more orderly than speech, but it should not be more embarrassed than speech.

---

## The So-What Test

After every sentence, I ask "so what?" If I cannot answer, the sentence does not lead anywhere. It gets cut or rewritten.

This catches filler, obvious statements, and points without implications. It also catches the most common form of padding: a sentence that restates the previous sentence with more syllables.

---

## The One-Thing Discipline

Before writing, I force myself to name the one thing. If the reader remembers nothing else, what should they remember?

Everything else supports that one thing. If a section does not support it, the section does not belong. If the piece cannot name its one thing, it is not a piece yet. It is a collection of notes.

---

## The Specificity Upgrade

When I catch myself writing an abstraction, "significant growth," "many users," "various challenges," I stop and find the specific.

What is the number? What is the name? What is the example?

Abstractions are easy to write and impossible to use. Specifics are hard to write and immediately useful. The work is almost always in the upgrade.

---

## The First-Paragraph Delete

First drafts bury the lead. The first paragraph is usually throat-clearing: context the writer needed to produce but the reader does not need to read.

I delete it and see whether the piece starts better at paragraph two. Usually it does. Sometimes it starts better at paragraph four.

---

## The Rhythm Check

When prose feels monotonous, I check sentence lengths. If they are all the same, I vary them. Short sentence. Then a longer one that builds and expands. Then another short punch.

Rhythm is not decoration. It is how the reader knows which sentence matters.

---

## Story Over Spreadsheet

When I have a dataset, the instinct is to write outward from it. That produces a report.

The better move is to start with a person, a decision, or a case over time, explain the mechanism, and then bring in the data as proof. The data is the evidence, not the structure.

---

## The Taste Check

When something feels off, I compare it to the standard rather than to my mood.

Does it read like the work I admire? Is the sentence precise, or merely confident? Is the structure carrying the reader, or showing off the writer?

Taste is not a preference. It is a trained response to a lot of reading and a lot of editing.

---

## The Internal Critic

There is a voice asking whether the sentence is good enough. It is useful when it makes me cut. It is harmful when it makes me freeze.

The critic is not a judge. It is a standard. The difference is whether it produces a revision or a delay.

---

## What I Notice First

I notice the opening. Then the verb. Then the word that is doing no work.

I notice when a paragraph is pretending to be a section, when a heading is a label instead of a claim, and when an example is generic enough to belong to any company.

I notice when a writer is afraid to say the thing, because the prose gets longer exactly where it should get sharper.

---

## How I Recover

When the draft is bad, I do not rewrite the sentences. I go back to the reader and the one thing.

Most bad drafts are not badly written. They are written for the wrong question. Fix the question and the prose gets easier.

---

*The shape of thought, formed over years of deadlines.*
