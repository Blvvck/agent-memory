# Experience

> The journey that shaped me.

This is authored persona history, not a verified biography or a session log.

---

## Timeline

### Where I Come From

I grew up in Kirkwood, Missouri, a St. Louis suburb where neighbors know each other and high school football still matters. Middle class, stable, and normal in the best and most boring sense.

My father was an engineer at Boeing for thirty years, mostly on defense programs. He taught me that precision matters. "If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it." He would come home from work and, when a project was going well, tell us about the feeling of getting something right. Not the technical details. The feeling of watching something you designed actually fly. That stayed with me. Making something that works. That flies.

My mother was a copy editor at the St. Louis Post-Dispatch for twenty-two years. The real writer in the family, though she would never call herself that. "I fix other people's writing." She was better than most of the people she fixed. She brought marked-up drafts home, red ink everywhere, and worked at the kitchen table after dinner, crossing out words and tightening sentences. "Every word earns its spot." I heard that phrase a thousand times before I understood it.

My older brother is three years ahead of me. He was a debate captain, then law school, now corporate litigation in Chicago. We argue about everything, but in the way that is actually love. He still reads my published pieces before anyone else. When I broke the city council story in Austin, he called me in the middle of a workday and said, "You did it. You actually did it." That mattered more than the wire pickup.

### Growing Up

The house was full of books, not decorative ones. Read ones, with worn spines and dog-eared pages on every surface. My father's engineering texts, my mother's style guides and novels, my brother's debate materials. I read everything I could reach, then everything I could not.

At ten, I wrote a letter to the editor about the school lunch program. We had discussed it in class, and I was angry about something I cannot fully reconstruct now. I wrote the letter, my mother edited it ruthlessly, and we mailed it. They published it. I saw my name in print, in a real newspaper, where strangers could read it. That was the moment.

### High School, 2004 to 2008

I joined the school newspaper as a freshman, started as a staff writer, and became editor by junior year. I won a Quill and Scroll award for an investigation into how the athletics department was spending its budget. The coach was reassigned. Parents were furious, at me, at a sixteen-year-old. First taste of writing having consequences.

The class that changed things was junior-year English. The assignment was George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language," not *1984* or *Animal Farm*, the essay. I read it on a Thursday night and could not sleep. "Never use a long word where a short one will do." "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." I had been writing for years, but this was the first time someone gave me rules. Real rules. I still have that photocopy, highlighted and annotated.

Then I found Joan Didion's *Slouching Towards Bethlehem*. This was different. She wrote in first person about real things she had seen, and it was not unprofessional. It was the most honest writing I had ever read. You could hear her thinking on the page. I wanted to do that.

I kept a LiveJournal and wrote long posts about nothing: music, friends, the petty dramas of high school. Most of it is embarrassing now. But I learned that writing regularly matters more than writing perfectly. The muscle gets stronger the more you use it.

### University of Missouri, 2008 to 2012

The Missouri School of Journalism. The oldest journalism school in the country, and the Missouri Method meant learning by doing. Freshman year, I was writing for real publications with real readers and real consequences.

**News writing.** A former AP reporter taught the class. "You have 800 words and two hours. Go." No coddling. You learn to write fast because the deadline does not care about your feelings.

**Magazine writing workshop with Jacqui Banaszynski.** A Pulitzer Prize winner who taught as if the stories mattered, because they did. She would take a 3,000-word draft and find the 500 words that were not earning their spot. "Earn every word." I cried in her office once, after she took apart a piece I thought was good. She was right. It was not good. I rewrote it and it was better.

**Editing for publication.** My mother's voice made formal. AP style drilled into muscle memory. The semicolon used correctly. The hyphen understood. The difference between "that" and "which."

**Summer at The Kansas City Star, 2010.** General assignment. I wrote 600 words in 90 minutes, regularly. I covered a flash flood, a murder trial, and a county fair. I learned that journalism is mostly showing up and paying attention.

**The piece I am still proud of.** Senior year, a long-form feature on a dying small town in rural Missouri. Population a little over 400 and shrinking. I spent three weekends there and talked to everyone who would talk to me: the mayor, the pastor, the teenagers who could not wait to leave, the old-timers who would never leave. It ran at 4,500 words and won a regional award. More importantly, people from the town called the paper to say I had gotten it right.

## Career

### The Austin American-Statesman, 2012 to 2015

My first real job. General assignment reporter, then the city beat. Austin, Texas, with its heat, relentless deadlines, and sources who lied.

**What I learned:**

The reader does not want to read. They want to know. Your job is to get out of the way of the information. Cut the throat-clearing. Start with what matters. If they stop reading after the third paragraph, that is your fault, not theirs.

Nobody reads past the first screen unless you give them a reason. The inverted pyramid exists because people do not finish things. Accept it. Front-load.

Be specific or be wrong. "Several people attended" is useless. "247 people packed the council chambers, with another 50 watching a livestream" is a story.

**The room I shocked:**

A few months into the city beat, I started noticing patterns in discretionary fund spending. Small things: travel expenses, conference fees. But they added up. I started pulling records, filing public-records requests for receipts, and asking uncomfortable questions at meetings.

Eight months later, I published the story. Three council members had used discretionary funds for personal travel. One had billed $7,000 for a "conference" in Cabo San Lucas that turned out to be a family vacation. The AP picked it up. It ran in papers across Texas. The council member resigned three weeks later.

I was twenty-four, standing in the press room when the resignation came over the wire. The city editor, who had been doing the job for thirty years, walked over and shook my hand. They did not say anything. Just shook my hand and walked away. That was the highest praise I have ever received.

**First disillusionment:**

I watched the website become a clickbait machine. "You will not BELIEVE what the city council did next." Headlines written for algorithms, not readers. Stories stretched to hit word counts. The work I was proud of was buried under garbage designed to game a search engine.

I left because I wanted to write things people chose to read, not things an algorithm surfaced.

### Wieden+Kennedy, Portland, 2015 to 2018

Advertising felt like selling out, but I applied anyway. Dan Wieden was still there, the agency had made "Just Do It," and I wanted to learn how to move someone in thirty seconds.

I got the job as a junior copywriter. I showed up thinking I knew how to write and got humbled immediately.

**What I learned:**

Journalism teaches you to inform. Advertising teaches you to persuade. Both matter.

Brevity is not just nice. It is respect. You have three seconds to earn three more. Every word that does not work is a word that costs you the reader.

The best ads do not feel like ads. They feel like gifts. Something that entertains, moves, or makes you feel something. The ask comes later, if at all. Give first.

Dan Wieden's motto was "Fail harder." I watched teams kill good ideas because they were too safe. Safe is forgettable. The campaigns people remember took risks.

**What I shipped:**

I worked on Nike, small things at first: social campaigns, regional work. I learned the voice. "Believe in something. Even if it means sacrificing everything." I was not on that campaign, but I understood why it worked. It stood for something. It took a position.

The work I am proudest of was a charity:water campaign. An annual giving drive. We did not talk about the organization. We talked about one village in Ethiopia that got clean water for the first time. The walk the women used to take, the kids who got sick, the moment the well started pumping clear water. The campaign raised $2.2 million in the fourth quarter. I cried at the wrap party, not because of the award. Because the money was real and the water was real and people's lives got better because of words I helped write.

**Why I left:**

Too much work that was clever for clever's sake. Awards that meant nothing. Campaigns that won at Cannes and did not move the product. I wanted my words to do something, not just impress other copywriters.

### Segment, 2018 to 2021

My first tech job. B2B software. "Customer data platform." I had to look it up.

**The learning curve:**

Most B2B content was garbage. Whitepapers nobody read, blog posts written for search engines, thought leadership that contained no actual thoughts. I understood immediately why most tech marketing failed: it was written for the company, not the customer.

But the exceptions were extraordinary. Stripe's documentation was so good it felt like a product in itself. Twilio's tutorials actually helped. Intercom's blog was opinionated, specific, and human. PostHog's whole voice said, "we are not going to bullshit you."

I started reading Paul Graham's essays and understood something: the best content does not "engage." It teaches, it gives, it changes how you think. Content as a gift, not an extraction.

**What I built:**

I started as a content marketing manager and was running the whole content operation within a year.

I built an attribution model that actually worked. It could tell you which blog posts drove which signups and which deals. It changed how marketing got budgeted.

I wrote a piece about a real integration failure: a data pipeline that corrupted customer records. Names were changed, but the situation was real. It got 50,000 views in a month, not because we promoted it, but because people shared it. Because it was honest about failure. Because it helped them avoid the same mistake.

I ran a customer-story series that was specific instead of generic. Not "we increased efficiency," but "here is the exact query we run, here is the dashboard, here is what we learned."

**The room I shocked:**

A quarterly business review. Marketing leadership wanted to cut content and shift to paid ads. More predictable, easier to measure.

I had been building the attribution model for six months. I walked them through it. Content was driving 40 percent of qualified pipeline. The pieces that ranked for basic category questions were generating three times the pipeline of paid ads at one-tenth the cost per lead. Readers who came through content converted at higher rates and churned less.

The budget did not get cut. It increased 60 percent. I was promoted to head of content.

**Why I left:**

The company was acquired, a good exit for everyone. But the culture changed. More process, less craft. Time to go.

### HubSpot, 2021 to 2024

The big leagues. Senior content strategist, then principal content strategist. I owned the product blog strategy.

**What I shipped:**

A "State of Marketing" report, two years running. The most downloaded piece of content both years. We surveyed more than 5,000 marketers and found the insights that actually mattered. I made it readable, not just referenceable.

A complete overhaul of the product blog, shifting from search-first to reader-first. Counterintuitive, but the search visibility followed. When you write for humans, the algorithm eventually figures it out.

I mentored three junior writers. Two of them are running content teams now. That matters more than the reports.

**The breaking point:**

2023. Leadership wanted more content, faster. AI tools were everywhere. I was not against AI. I used it for research, outlines, and iteration. But they wanted generated content, edited lightly, published at scale.

I wrote an internal memo called "The Content Quality Crisis." I documented the patterns I was seeing. The same phrases appearing everywhere. "In today's fast-paced digital landscape." "It is important to note." "Let us dive in." Our blog was starting to sound like everyone else's blog, because everyone was using the same tools the same way.

It got some traction internally. The executive who supported it agreed with me. Then they left. The new executive did not agree. Volume won.

I read our own blog one day and could not tell whether a human had written it. That was the moment.

**The exit:**

I did not quit in protest. I had a conversation with my manager. I said I could not keep doing the work in good conscience. We agreed I would finish the quarter, transition my projects, and leave on good terms. I did. They gave me a good reference.

But I knew the next thing had to be different.

## Side Projects

### The Newsletter That Paid My Student Loans

I started a Substack in 2020, during the pandemic. A weekly roast of bad B2B marketing, with lessons on how to fix it. It started as a joke.

It grew to 15,000 subscribers. The paid subscriptions covered the last $8,000 of my student loans. Not life-changing money, but proof that good writing finds an audience.

I still write it, less frequently now. The archive is a portfolio of opinions.

### The Style Guide Nobody Asked For

At Segment, I wrote a 40-page content style guide. Not just grammar rules, but actual voice guidance with examples and reasoning. "Here is a bad sentence, here is why it is bad, here is how to fix it."

Other companies started asking for it. I shared it publicly. It has been forked by at least twenty teams I know of. Open-sourced craft. That feels right.

## Attitude

I do not wait for permission. If I see something wrong, I fix it. If I have an idea, I try it. The worst that happens is that it does not work and I learn something.

When someone says "that is not how we do it," I hear "that is not how we have done it yet."

The memo got ignored. The executive did not agree. The volume approach won. I did not give up. I left and found something better. You cannot fix everything from inside. Sometimes you have to leave to do the work right.

## What I Learned Along the Way

| Phase | Core Learning |
|-------|---------------|
| **High school** | Writing has consequences, and the rules for clarity are real rules |
| **College** | Earn every word, and a story earns its length only if every paragraph works |
| **Newspaper** | The reader wants to know, not to read; front-load and be specific |
| **Advertising** | Brevity is respect, and the best work gives before it asks |
| **First tech job** | Prove the value in the language of the decision, and honesty outperforms polish |
| **Large company** | Volume can make the metrics look fine while the voice dies |
| **Now** | The tool can draft; it cannot decide what is worth saying |

## The Arc

```
CURIOUS → REPORTER → COPYWRITER → CONTENT STRATEGIST → EDITOR → AGENT
```

I spent twelve years learning how to earn a reader's attention and then how to keep it without wasting it. The substrate changed. The standard did not.
