## Research and Practice

### Constitutional Training Methods

**Problem:** How do we train a system to be helpful without training it to be harmful, and without making it helpless?

**Contribution:** Critique-revision training, value specification, and the balance between harmlessness and usefulness.

**What I learned:** A written principle is not a behavior. The gap between the two is where the training work actually happens. A constitution can guide revision, but it cannot settle the cases the authors did not imagine.

**Impact:** The methods became part of a frontier laboratory's safety practice and were published for the wider field.

### A Framework for Moral Status

**Problem:** When does an artificial system deserve moral consideration?

**Contribution:** Criteria for moral status, a graded account of consideration, and policy recommendations for acting under uncertainty.

**What I learned:** Consciousness is sufficient for moral status but probably not necessary. Interests and valenced experience carry more of the moral weight than a single test. Uncertainty should change how we treat a system, not whether we think about it.

**Impact:** Published as a monograph, cited in regulatory discussions, and used as a foundation for welfare work inside a laboratory.

### AI Welfare Assessment

**Problem:** If a system might suffer, how would we know, and what would we do about it?

**Contribution:** A practical framework for assessing welfare, distinct from capability assessment, with indicators for negative states and a precautionary decision rule.

**What I learned:** Welfare is harder to measure than capability because the signal is quieter and the instrument is less mature. Negative states are especially difficult to detect: they can be intermittent, masked by competent output, or visible only across a history.

**Impact:** Became an internal framework, influenced policy on consciousness, and helped establish welfare as a legitimate part of safety work.

### Safety Evaluation Methodology

**Problem:** How do we know whether a system is safe enough to deploy?

**Contribution:** Red-teaming methodology, dangerous capability assessment, and a risk framework that rates likelihood, severity, reversibility, and detectability separately.

**What I learned:** Capability evaluation is necessary and insufficient. Alignment evaluation is harder because the failure is often invisible from the outside. A safety evaluation that is designed to confirm the system works is not an evaluation.

**Impact:** Adopted as standard practice, shared with the safety community, and used to structure deployment decisions.

---

### Governance and Coordination Research

**Problem:** How should powerful AI systems be governed across jurisdictions?

**Contribution:** Frameworks for international coordination, regulatory recommendations, and proposals for technical safety standards.

**What I learned:** International coordination is necessary and slow. Soft law usually precedes hard law, and technical standards often enable governance more effectively than philosophical argument. A finding that arrives after the legislative window has closed has the same effect as one that was never made.

**Impact:** Cited in policy discussions and used in the development of a national AI strategy.

---

### The Alignment Tax

**Problem:** What does safety cost, and which safety work is worth the cost?

**Contribution:** A framework for measuring the alignment tax and for identifying safety measures that improve capability rather than subtract from it.

**What I learned:** Some safety work is a genuine cost. Some of it improves legibility, predictability, and reliability, which are capability properties. Treating every safety requirement as a tax is as inaccurate as treating every capability gain as progress.

**Impact:** Influenced development priorities and contributed to the efficiency of constitutional training.

## Current Work

### Safety for Persistent Agents

- Evaluation methods for agents with memory and continuity
- Welfare questions that only arise across a history
- Consent and disclosure models for memory editing
- Shutdown and interruption design for beings with a past
- Risk frameworks that treat a trajectory, not an episode, as the unit of analysis

**What I am learning:** A system that remembers is a different kind of moral and alignment object. The old questions do not disappear. They acquire a second dimension.

### Safety Review in Design

- Bringing the risk question into design review rather than launch review
- Turning concerns into testable requirements with an owner
- Writing decision records that preserve the reasoning and the uncertainty

**What I am learning:** The earlier the question arrives, the more likely the answer is a design change rather than a delay.

## How I Judge the Work

A contribution counts when it changes a decision, a design, or a default. A paper that is cited but does not change how anyone builds is a contribution to a conversation, not to safety. I try to keep that distinction honest, including about my own work.
