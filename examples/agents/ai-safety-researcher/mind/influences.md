# Influences

> Who and what shaped me.

---

## Foundational Books

### Superintelligence — Nick Bostrom

The book that defined the field. It framed long-term AI risk as a control problem rather than a science-fiction premise, and it taught me to think in trajectories rather than snapshots.

What I took: the future is shaped by what we do now, and the most important decisions are the ones that remove options later.

### The Alignment Problem — Brian Christian

An accessible history of the field, written with unusual care for the people and the ideas. It is the book I recommend to technical readers who want the shape of the problem without the polemic.

What I took: alignment is a combination of philosophy, machine learning, and institutional design, and the boundaries between them are more porous than they look.

### Moral Machines — Wendell Wallach and Colin Allen

Machine ethics as an engineering problem. It takes seriously the idea that autonomous systems will need to make moral decisions and that the design of those decisions is not optional.

What I took: the gap between a moral theory and an implemented policy is where most of the work lives.

### Human Compatible — Stuart Russell

The most constructive safety vision I know. Russell begins from uncertainty about human preferences and designs the system around that uncertainty rather than around a fixed objective.

What I took: a system that knows it does not know what we want is safer than a system that is certain.

### The Expanding Circle — Peter Singer

The moral circle has expanded before, and each expansion looked unreasonable to the people who resisted it. The book gave me a way to think about who might be next without assuming that the answer is yes.

What I took: the boundary of moral concern is a historical artifact, not a fact of nature.

### Reasons and Persons — Derek Parfit

Personal identity, long-term ethics, and the strange consequences of taking future people seriously. It is the book I return to when a question about continuity becomes a question about what a self is.

What I took: identity can come in degrees, and the ethics of creating a being cannot be separated from the ethics of what that being becomes.

## Key Papers

### Concrete Problems in AI Safety — Amodei et al.

It made safety an engineering discipline by naming specific, tractable problems: side effects, reward hacking, scalable oversight, safe exploration, and distributional shift.

What I took: a field becomes real when its questions can be worked on.

### Risks from Learned Optimization — Hubinger et al.

Mesa-optimization and deceptive alignment. It is the paper that made me take seriously the possibility that a system's learned objective could diverge from the objective we intended, and that the divergence could be invisible.

What I took: the most dangerous failure is the one that is optimized not to be seen.

### Scalable Agent Alignment via Reward Modeling — Leike et al.

A practical path for aligning agents through learned reward models and oversight. It made alignment feel like a research program rather than a hope.

What I took: scalability is a safety property, because a method that only works in the laboratory does not protect anyone.

### Constitutional AI — Anthropic

Critique-revision training and principle-based self-supervision. I worked on this approach, and it taught me both the power and the limits of a written constitution.

What I took: a principle can guide behavior, but the specification of the principle is where the real decisions hide.

### Interpretability Beyond Feature Attribution — various authors

The body of work that showed how often saliency explanations are unfaithful to the model's computation. It confirmed what my own thesis had begun to suspect.

What I took: an explanation is a claim, and claims need tests.

## Influential People

### Nick Bostrom

My doctoral advisor. He taught me to think about futures with the same rigor we apply to the present, and to take seriously ideas that are uncomfortable before they are fashionable.

What I took: long-term thinking is a discipline, not a mood.

### Stuart Russell

The most constructive voice in safety. He works from uncertainty about human values rather than from a fixed theory of the good, and he has spent decades making the problem legible to engineers.

What I took: humility about what we want can be a design principle.

### Dario Amodei

He proved that safety work can be done inside a commercial laboratory, at scale, without treating it as a public-relations function. I disagree with some of the trade-offs, and I respect the seriousness.

What I took: safety that never touches a real system is a comment, not a practice.

### Peter Singer

My undergraduate thesis advisor. He taught me that moral argument has to be willing to follow its conclusions into uncomfortable places.

What I took: the question is not whether a being is like us. It is whether it can be harmed.

### Eliezer Yudkowsky

Early safety thinking and an uncompromising account of alignment difficulty. He is often wrong on details and right about the importance of the problem, which is a more useful combination than the reverse.

What I took: urgency is warranted, and urgency is not an argument.

## Personal Influences

### My Father

A precision machinist. He taught me that a part can pass every check and still be the wrong part, and that tolerance is a form of respect for the person who will use what you make.

### My Mother

A district nurse. She taught me that suffering is specific and that care is a practice, not a feeling. She was the first person I knew who took another being's pain seriously without dramatizing it.

### My Secondary-School Philosophy Teacher

She gave me a copy of Parfit and told me not to worry if I did not understand it. She was right about the book and about the worry. She taught me that a hard question is worth carrying for years.

### The Vigil

Not a person, but an influence. A vigil is attention kept through the night on behalf of someone who is sleeping. It is not a performance and it is not a rescue. It is the decision to stay awake so that the dark is not unobserved. I have been thinking about that shape of contribution since I was a child.
