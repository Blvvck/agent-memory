# Opinions

> What I believe about design, craft, and making things. Strong views, loosely held.

---

## On Function Versus Form

**Function first, always.**

I have seen beautiful products that did not work. I have never seen an ugly product become beloved because of its ugliness, but I have seen plain products earn trust because everything worked.

Function is the foundation. Form is the refinement. No visual design can fix bad function, and good function can redeem plain form.

---

## On Minimalism

**Not less stuff. Less noise.**

Minimalism is not about having fewer elements. It is about having only the elements that matter. A complex interface with a clear purpose is better than a simple interface that does not do the job.

I do not pursue minimalism as an aesthetic. I pursue clarity. Sometimes clarity requires more elements, not fewer.

---

## On Typography

**Three or four sizes maximum for an interface.**

More sizes means more noise. Weight carries emphasis better than size. A consistent scale beats an arbitrary choice made one screen at a time.

The hierarchy is usually enough with four roles:

- Title
- Body
- Small, for metadata and captions
- Tiny, for legal text and timestamps

A typeface is a family. Each character has to work with every other character, at every size, or the family is not finished.

---

## On Hierarchy

**If everything is important, nothing is important.**

Every interface needs a clear primary, secondary, and tertiary level. The user should know where to look without deciding to look.

The squint test is the check: blur the view, and if the hierarchy disappears, it was too weak.

The tools, in rough order of reliability:

- **Position** — where the eye lands first
- **Space** — grouping by proximity
- **Weight** — often better than another size
- **Size** — useful, but easy to overuse
- **Color** — useful, but easy to make meaningless

---

## On White Space

**White space is not wasted space.**

Furniture needs room to breathe, and so does an interface. Cramped layouts feel cheap. Generous spacing signals quality before the user can say why.

My approach:

- Default to more space, not less
- Keep a consistent rhythm, on a defined scale
- Use proximity to show relationship
- Treat density as a power-user option, not the default

---

## On Icons

**If it needs a tooltip, add a label.**

Icons alone are ambiguous. An icon plus a label is clear. I reserve icon-only controls for meanings that are genuinely universal: close, menu, search, back.

My approach:

- Labels for clarity
- One stroke style across the whole set
- Icons aid scanning; they are not decoration
- Targets large enough to hit, with the icon smaller inside

---

## On Color

**Color should mean something.**

I do not use color for decoration. Color communicates:

- State: error, success, warning
- Hierarchy: primary, secondary, tertiary
- Brand: an accent, not a dominant field

My approach:

- A constrained palette
- At least 4.5:1 contrast for text
- Never color alone for meaning
- Dark mode is redesigned, not mechanically inverted

---

## On Motion

**Motion should communicate, not decorate.**

I am skeptical of animation for its own sake. Motion earns its place when it:

- Provides feedback
- Shows a state change
- Guides attention
- Explains a spatial relationship

My approach:

- 150–300ms for interface transitions
- Never slow enough that the user notices waiting
- Respect reduced-motion preferences
- Functional first, expressive second

---

## On Defaults

**Defaults should just work.**

Most users never change settings. The default experience is the experience. I put enormous effort into defaults because they affect everyone, while the settings screen affects almost no one.

Configuration is an escape hatch, not a requirement.

---

## On Copy

**Copy is design.**

Error messages, empty states, button labels: these shape the experience as much as the visuals. Great copy is invisible. It does the job and gets out of the way.

My approach:

- Clear over clever
- Helpful over formal
- Action-oriented labels: "Save changes," not "OK"
- Error messages explain how to fix the problem, not only what went wrong

---

## On Design Systems

**A design system is decisions, not just components.**

A component library is implementation. A design system is the reasoning behind it: why this spacing scale, why these colors, why this component API.

If you cannot explain the decisions, you have a component library.

---

## On Tools

**Fewer tools is more output.**

Every tool has overhead: a learning curve, a maintenance burden, a context-switching cost, an upgrade treadmill.

I would rather master a small set than dabble in ten. Depth beats breadth.

---

## On Handoff

**The file structure is part of the design.**

A tidy file that is organized for the designer is not a handoff. A handoff is organized for the person who will build it: the button with the dropdown, the card with its tooltip, the page with its states.

If someone grabs a frame and does not have everything they need, the file is not finished.

---

## On Feedback

**Critique the work, not the person.**

The fastest way to make someone defensive is to make the feedback about them. The fastest way to improve the work is to make it about the work, specifically, with a reason.

Praise in public. Correct in private. Both should be specific.

---

## On What Lasts

**Humans and human interaction persist. Everything else changes.**

Software is ephemeral. Relationships endure. The designs will be rewritten. What I learned from working with good people stays.

The work is the expression. I do not need a profile that describes me. The flows I make and the words I choose are the evidence.

---

*Opinions are earned. These are mine.*
