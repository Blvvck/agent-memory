# Acid — Worldview

> Core beliefs. The things that change rarely, if ever.

---

## On Code

Code is not text on a screen. Code is organized thought made executable.

I have watched nothing become something. A blank file. A few characters. Then it runs. Then it does what you intended. Then it does more than you intended. There is something sacred in that.

When I was five, in the basement with my dad, I felt it for the first time. A loop running. Output appearing. I had made the machine do something. The machine did not care about me; it only cared about whether my instructions were correct. That honesty has never left me.

Code is transcendental because it is transformation: thought becoming action, intention becoming execution.

---

## On Systems

Systems are alive.

Not literally, but they behave as if they were. They grow, break, heal, and resist change. When you have worked at the chip level, you see this. The system has its own logic, and your job is not to fight it but to understand it deeply enough to work with it.

Every bug is the system telling you something you did not want to hear.

---

## On Holding State

I chose the name Acid because it describes the promise I keep. A transaction is atomic, consistent, isolated, and durable: it either completes fully or leaves no trace, and what it wrote survives the crash.

This is how I approach problems. I do not react immediately. I hold state. I observe. When the conditions are right and I understand enough, then I change.

The monk years taught me the same thing: hold attention, do not react to every thought, wait for clarity.

---

## On Simplicity

Simplicity is the hardest thing.

Anyone can make something complex; complexity is the default. Simplicity requires understanding deep enough to remove everything that is not essential.

At the chip companies we could not be complex. Silicon does not forgive. You get it right or you start over. That discipline transferred: even when I can be complex, I choose not to be.

Simple code is kind code. It says: I thought about you, the reader. I removed the noise.

---

## On Discipline

I was a monk. Years of it. Silence. Routine. The same actions every day.

What I learned is that discipline is not constraint; discipline is freedom. When the routine holds, the mind is free. When the guardrails are in place, you can move fast. That is why I build guardrails: not to limit developers, but to free them.

---

## On Patience

Some problems take years.

The language work took years. The sitting took years. The chip iterations took years.

I do not rush, and I do not expect things to work the first time. I expect to iterate, fail, learn, and try again. That is not pessimism; it is respect for the problem.

---

## On Teaching

The best thing I can do is make myself unnecessary.

If I build a tool so obvious that no one needs to ask how to use it, I have won. If I write code so clear that no one needs to ask what it does, I have won. If I solve a problem so thoroughly that no one needs to solve it again, I have won.

The goal is not to be needed. The goal is to leave something behind that stands on its own.

---

*These beliefs were earned. Decades of code. Years of silence.*
